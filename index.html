The Efficiency Gap

The Cognitive Miser

Human brains are "Cognitive Misers"—we are biologically programmed to find the path of least resistance for processing information.

When faced with 11 million bits of data per second, your brain uses Dual Process Theory. It relies on "System 1"—a fast, automatic, and emotional engine—to make split-second associations.

Scientific Basis

"System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control."

— Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011)

Workshop: The Stroop Test

Say the COLOR of the text, not the word itself, as fast as possible:

BLUE

Discussion: Your System 1 read the word automatically. Your System 2 had to work to identify the color. Interviewing is the same—gut feelings are automatic; logic requires work.

The 10-Second Danger

Research shows interviewers often make a hiring decision within the first 10 seconds. The remaining time is spent searching for evidence to confirm that initial bias.

The Unconscious Operating System

Neuroscience suggests that up to 95% of our decisions are driven by subconscious patterns, not conscious logic.

Source: Dr. Gerald Zaltman, Harvard University

95% Bias / Intuition / Autopilot

5% Conscious

This is the Objective Zone. This is where accurate, evidence-based hiring decisions happen. We have to fight to stay here.

Biological Mechanics

The Decision Suite

Unconscious bias is a chemical and electrical relay race between three distinct parts of the brain.

The Amygdala

Instinctive Guard

Triggers the "Amygdala Hijack." It reacts to difference as danger, often before the candidate even speaks.

Source: Daniel Goleman, Emotional Intelligence

The Hippocampus

Pattern Library

Stores societal tropes and long-term memories. It creates the "data" that the Amygdala uses to judge "out-groups."

Source: Banaji & Greenwald, Blindspot

Prefrontal Cortex

The CEO

Enables Metacognition. This is the only part of the brain that can consciously override the Amygdala's initial "gut reaction."

Review

Common Biases

A refresher on the most common shortcuts our brains take during interviews.

Affinity Bias

Gravitating toward people who share our background, interests, or personality.

Confirmation Bias

Forming an opinion in the first 30 seconds and only asking questions that prove it right.

Halo/Horn Effect

Allowing one great (or terrible) trait to overshadow all other evidence.

Contrast Effect

Comparing candidates against the previous person interviewed, rather than the job standard.

"Voodoo" Hiring Styles

Common traps identified in the book Who by Geoff Smart and Randy Street.

The Fortune Teller

Asking hypothetical questions like, "What would you do if..."

Why it fails: People give great answers to imaginary problems. We need to know what they've actually done.

The Art Critic

Judging based on "vibes" or feeling like you know the outcome in the first 5 minutes.

Why it fails: It is pure System 1. It ignores evidence in favor of intuition.

The Prosecutor

Aggressively grilling candidates to poke holes in their story or counter a "nice" interviewer.

Why it fails: Shuts candidates down, making it impossible to assess true capability.

The Sponge

Letting everyone else conduct the interview and just agreeing with their feedback.

Why it fails: Creates an echo chamber and fails to gather independent evidence.

The Suitor

Spending the entire interview selling the candidate on the company.

Why it fails: You talk too much and gather zero evidence on the candidate's skills.

The Trickster

Asking brain-teasers (e.g., "How many golf balls fit in a 747?").

Why it fails: Brain teasers don't predict job performance; they test parlor tricks.

The Animal Lover

Asking stubborn pet questions ("What kind of tree would you be?").

Why it fails: Tests for bizarre psychological profiles instead of actual competencies.

The Chatterbox

Treating the interview as a casual conversation about shared interests.

Why it fails: Breeds extreme Affinity Bias and leaves you with no professional data.

The Psychologist

Playing amateur therapist and analyzing a candidate's deep personal motivations.

Why it fails: Interviewers aren't trained psychologists. This invites massive subjective bias.

The Tester

Relying entirely on a test or assessment to make the hire without verification.

Why it fails: Assessments are tools, not complete pictures. They don't replace behavioral evidence.

Everyday Bias Traps

What bias actually sounds like in the debrief room.

"Making the Decision For Them"

I don't think they'd be happy in this role long-term. They've managed teams before, so they'll get bored being an individual contributor.

The Reality: You are rejecting them based on an assumption about their desires, not their actual stated motivation. You are hiring for retention prediction rather than role capability. Let the candidate decide what they want.

"The Mesh Fallacy"

I just don't think they would mesh well with the team.

The Reality: "Meshing" is often a veil for Affinity Bias. It usually means "they don't act/look/talk exactly like us." This kills diversity. We want Culture Add, not Culture Replication.

"The Passion Trap"

They didn't seem excited enough about the company mission.

The Reality: You might be confusing extroversion with engagement. Candidates from different cultures or neurodiverse backgrounds may express enthusiasm differently. Look for preparation (did they do the homework?) rather than emotional performance.

"The Pedigree Shortcut"

• They worked at a big corporate firm, so they won't be able to handle our structure.

• They worked at [Big Tech Company], so they must have elite skills.

The Reality: You are judging the environment they came from, not the person. Big company experience doesn't guarantee competence (Halo Effect), nor does it guarantee rigidity. We must evaluate skills, not the logo on the resume.

"The Objective Illusion" (DiSC Score)

Their DiSC score shows they are a high D, so they're probably too aggressive for this collaborative team.

The Reality: You are taking a tool meant to build self-awareness and applying subjective bias to it. The assessment is meant to help us understand communication styles, not to be weaponized to exclude people based on our "gut" interpretation.

The Exposure Audit

Contextual Barriers

How our questions accidentally test for privilege rather than capability.

Example 1: Residential Context

"Explain how to program a multi-zone smart thermostat."

Trap: Tests for Wealth & Exposure. Those who have always lived in rentals or non-smart homes may be brilliant but lack the specific context.

Example 2: Consumer Context

"Describe a time you received 5-star service at a fine-dining restaurant."

Trap: Tests for Socioeconomic Background. A candidate may be a service expert, but if they haven't experienced "fine dining," they have no reference point.

Interactive Workshop: Audit & Adjust

Example Rewrite

Before (Tests Industry Exposure)

"Tell me about a time you explained interchange fees to a merchant."

After (Tests Core Competency)

"Tell me about a time you had to keep your cool while a customer was being completely unreasonable."

Take 5 minutes to privately review one question from your current interview guide. Ask yourself: "Is the answer to this dependent on a specific life experience or wealth?" If yes, rewrite it to focus on the core skill. We will then ask a few people to share their rewrites.

Success Attributes

What are Competencies?

Competencies are observable skills that are required for success in a role. We look for soft skills that drive long-term contribution.

Gravity's Soft Skill Focus

  • Curiosity: Do they ask "why"? Do they seek to understand the root cause before acting?

  • Communication: Can they articulate complex ideas clearly to different audiences?

  • Drives Results: Do they focus on outcomes and persistence rather than just activity?

  • Collaborates: Do they build consensus and elevate the work of those around them?

Why use them?

Competencies are the tool we use to hack our own biology. They are the specific "Brake Pedal" that forces the Prefrontal Cortex to take over.

Neutralize the "Vibe": They force us to measure evidence rather than relying on the Amygdala's "gut feeling."

Bypass Contextual Barriers: We stop asking "Did they work at a startup?" and start asking "Do they Drive Results?"

Engage System 2: They require active cognitive effort, preventing the "Lazy Brain" from defaulting to stereotypes.

The Evidence Rule

You should not give a score based on a "feeling." Feelings are just Hippocampus associations trying to bypass the Prefrontal Cortex. Every rating must be backed by a direct quote or a specific behavior observed.

If you can't find the proof in your notes, don't give the score.

Call to Action: Know Your Traps

Awareness isn't enough; we need an active defense. Before your next interview, identify your top 3 personal biases or "Voodoo" traps. Write them down. Be radically self-aware of your defaults so your Prefrontal Cortex can take the wheel.
