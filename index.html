The Biology of Shortcuts

Bias is Biology, Not a Failing

The Cognitive Miser

Biases are a natural part of human biology. Our brains process about 11 million bits of data per second, which is exhausting. To survive, the brain uses Dual Process Theory.

It relies on "System 1"—a fast, automatic engine—to make split-second associations (like Birthday = Cake). The issue isn't having the shortcut; the issue is not being aware of it when judging candidates.

Scientific Basis

"System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control."

— Daniel Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow

Workshop: The Stroop Test

Say the COLOR of the text, not the word itself, as fast as possible:

BLUE

Discussion: Your System 1 read the word automatically. You have to be accountable and force your brain (System 2) to find the color. We must do the exact same thing when judging a candidate's skills.

The 10-Second Danger

Research shows interviewers often make a hiring decision within the first 10 seconds. The remaining time is spent searching for evidence to confirm that initial bias.

1. Awareness

Knowing that your brain is automatically trying to fill in the blanks about a candidate using past associations.

2. Recognition

Spotting it in the moment. Realizing, "I'm assuming they know this because of where they worked."

3. Accountability

Actively choosing to bypass the shortcut and seek out factual information through follow-up questions.

The Unconscious Operating System

Neuroscience suggests that up to 95% of our decisions are driven by subconscious patterns, not conscious logic.

Source: Dr. Gerald Zaltman, Harvard University

95% Bias / Intuition / Autopilot

5% Conscious

This is the Objective Zone. This is where accurate, evidence-based hiring decisions happen. We have to fight to stay here.

Biological Mechanics

The Decision Suite

Unconscious bias is a chemical and electrical relay race between three distinct parts of the brain.

The Amygdala

Instinctive Guard

Triggers the "Amygdala Hijack." It reacts to difference as danger, often before the candidate even speaks.

Source: Daniel Goleman, Emotional Intelligence

The Hippocampus

Pattern Library

Stores societal tropes and long-term memories. It creates the "data" that the Amygdala uses to judge "out-groups."

Source: Banaji & Greenwald, Blindspot

Prefrontal Cortex

The CEO

Enables Metacognition. This is the only part of the brain that can consciously override the Amygdala to seek factual information.

Everyday Traps in the Debrief Room

How the Voodoo styles show up in our language.

Root: The Fortune Teller / Psychologist

"Making the Decision For Them"

I don't think they'd be happy in this role long-term. They've managed teams before, so they'll get bored being an individual contributor.

The Reality: You are rejecting them based on an assumption about their desires. Let the candidate decide what they want; we hire for factual capability.

Root: The Art Critic / Chatterbox

"The Mesh Fallacy"

I just don't think they would mesh well with the team.

The Reality: "Meshing" is often a veil for affinity bias. It usually means "they don't act/look/talk exactly like us." We want Culture Add, not Culture Replication.

Root: The Art Critic

"The Passion Trap"

They didn't seem excited enough about the company mission.

The Reality: You might be confusing extroversion with engagement. Look for factual preparation (did they do the homework?) rather than emotional performance.

Root: The Tester / Sponge

"The Pedigree Shortcut"

• They worked at a big corporate firm, so they won't be able to handle our structure.

• They worked at [Big Tech Company], so they must have elite skills.

The Reality: You are judging the environment they came from, not the person. Big company experience guarantees neither competence nor rigidity.

The Exposure Audit

Contextual Barriers

How our questions accidentally test for privilege rather than capability.

Example 1: Residential Context

"Explain how to program a multi-zone smart thermostat."

Trap: Tests for Wealth & Exposure. Those who have always lived in rentals or non-smart homes may be brilliant but lack the specific context.

Example 2: Consumer Context

"Describe a time you received 5-star service at a fine-dining restaurant."

Trap: Tests for Socioeconomic Background. A candidate may be a service expert, but if they haven't experienced "fine dining," they have no reference point.

Interactive Workshop

The Interpretation Gap

How one question creates multiple realities, and how follow-up questions bring us back to factual information.

The Prompt

"Tell me about a time you showed scrappiness."

The Candidate Hears:
  • • "Tell me about a time you worked 80 hours."
  • • "Tell me about a time you had zero budget."
  • • "Tell me about a time you broke the rules."
The Fix: Verifying Questions

When you hear an answer based on interpretation, do not assume you know what they mean. Dig for factual information.

  • "What were the specific constraints?"
  • "Walk me through the steps you took."
  • "Who exactly did you collaborate with on that?"

Follow-up questions are how we break out of the traps. They force us to replace our assumptions with factual information.

The Strategy

Rely on Factual Information

We bypass our natural shortcuts by grounding our decisions in observable, factual evidence.

Gravity's Soft Skill Focus

Competencies are the specific "Brake Pedal" that forces the Prefrontal Cortex to take over. When we focus on these, we stop evaluating "vibes".

  • Curiosity: Do they ask "why"? Do they seek to understand the root cause before acting?

  • Communication: Can they articulate complex ideas clearly to different audiences?

  • Drives Results: Do they focus on outcomes and persistence rather than just activity?

  • Collaborates: Do they build consensus and elevate the work of those around them?

The Evidence Rule

You should not give a score based on a "feeling." Feelings are just automatic associations trying to bypass the critical thinking of your Prefrontal Cortex. Every rating must be backed by a direct quote or a specific, factual behavior observed.

If you can't find factual proof in your notes, don't give the score.

Accountability: Leave With A Plan

Look at your notes. Which "Voodoo Style" did you write down? What is your specific plan to overcome it using follow-up questions and factual information in your very next interview?
